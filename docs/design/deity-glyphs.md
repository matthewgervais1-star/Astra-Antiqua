# The deity glyphs

One mark per body per culture: fifty in all. These sit beside a body's name in
the chart wheel, in the placements table, and in running text, at roughly 16 to
24 px, monochrome, single weight. They are marks, not illustrations.

Researched by five parallel briefs, one per culture, each followed by an
adversarial verification pass whose first job was to catch a glyph claiming a
pedigree it does not have.

## The honesty rule, and how it held

Every entry carries a status:

- **attested.** this exact attribute is used for this deity in the real record.
- **composed.** the attribute is genuinely that deity's, but this reduction to
  a planetary sign is modern work.
- **invented.** no ancient basis. Made for this product, and labelled so.

The result across fifty entries: **28 attested, 21 composed, 1 invented.**

(An earlier version of this line read 27 and 22. The tables below are the
record, they were counted, and the summary was one out.)

The rule that mattered most: the three received bodies cannot have attested
glyphs, because the culture never knew the planet. All fifteen came back
composed or invented, none falsely attested. Osiris's crook and flail is
thoroughly attested for Osiris; nobody in Egypt ever used it for a planet,
because Pluto was found in 1930. The attribute is ancient, the assignment is
ours, and the app already says so on its method page.

The single invented glyph is Canaan's Shamem (Uranus), who survives as little
more than a name for "the heavens". Rather than dress an invention as a find,
it is drawn as two stacked horizontal bars and marked invented.

## What the classical planet symbols actually are

The familiar signs are Byzantine-era abstractions of Greek and Roman deity
attributes: the Venus sign is a hand mirror, the Mars sign a shield and spear,
the Saturn sign a harpe sickle. They are not neutral astronomical notation and
they are not ancient Egyptian or Canaanite in any sense. Using them for Egypt or
Canaan would be a category error, which is why this set does not.

For Greece and Rome the picture is different: those symbols descend from these
very attributes, so a Greek or Roman glyph that resembles the familiar sign is
being honest rather than lazy. The trident for Neptune is the clearest case. The
attribute is ancient and superbly attested; its use as a *planetary* sign dates
to 1846, when the planet was named. Hence composed, not attested.

## The overlaps, and which ones are real problems

Three kinds of overlap show up in the matrix. They need different answers.

### 1. The sun and moon are a disc and a crescent everywhere

Four of five cultures render the sun with a disc and the moon with a crescent.
This is not a failure of imagination, it is what the sun and moon look like. The
separation comes from the frame, not the shape: Ra's disc carries a uraeus,
Khonsu's sits in a crescent cup, Carthage's moon is the sign of Tanit. Leave it.

### 2. Greece and Rome share five subjects out of ten

Mars is a crested helmet in both. Jupiter is a thunderbolt in both. Saturn is the
harpe in both. Neptune is a trident in both. Mercury is the same herald's staff.

This is the honest answer, because Rome took these gods from Greece wholesale.
Forcing an artificial difference would be its own dishonesty, and the app's claim
was never that the gods differ. It is that the *readings* differ: Greece
questions, Rome rules.

**The resolution is the hand, not the subject.** Draw the Greek marks in one
stroke language and the Roman in another, taking the cue from where each is
actually attested: Greek attributes come to us mostly from vase painting, Roman
mostly from struck coin relief. Same trident, different hand, is the most
truthful visual statement of "Rome inherited this god and read him its own way."
This is a drawing instruction, and it is the one thing in this document that
cannot be resolved by choosing a different attribute.

### 3. Carthage and Rome both use a caduceus for Mercury

Sakun and Mercurius. The Punic caduceus-like staff is a real motif on Punic
stelae, but the icon research already flagged that it reads as Greek or medical
to a modern eye. With Rome using the caduceus properly, Carthage should move to
another attribute rather than hold a mark that is both ambiguous and duplicated.

## The fifty

Statuses below are normalised to the three-value scale. The attestation and
collision notes are the verifiers' own words, left as written. Where a verifier wrote a
longer justification, it is preserved in the notes under each culture.

**Within-culture collisions.** Three shapes needed active separation, plus one the original pass missed. (1) Discs: Ra, Khonsu, and Nun all carry a circular element. Resolved by giving each a different frame rather than a bare circle — Ra's disc sits alone beside a small uraeus hook, Khonsu's disc is caught inside a crescent 'cup,' and Nun's disc is the smallest and most optional of the three, held between raised arms; if any two still crowd at 16px, drop Nun's disc first since the raised-arms V-shape is independently legible without it (it is itself a separately attested Egyptian sign, the ka-arms). (2) Twin plumes: Amun's shuty crown and Osiris's atef crown both carry paired ostrich feathers in the full iconographic record. Resolved by not using the atef crown for Osiris at all — his entry uses crook-and-flail instead, so 'twin uprights' belongs to Amun alone in this set. (3) Crescents: Khonsu shares the crescent-moon motif with the god Yah elsewhere in the wider Egyptian record, but Yah is not among these ten deities, so no in-set collision actually arises from that pairing — flagged only in case a future roster adds him. (4) Arcs, added on review: Khonsu's crescent-cradling-disc and Nut's dot-studded arc are both 'curved line plus small round accent(s)' shapes, and the original collision note never checked them against each other — it only checked Khonsu against the out-of-set god Yah. Resolved by rewriting Nut's stroke spec: her arc must stay visibly shallower than Khonsu's deeper crescent cup, her dots sit on the arc's convex outer edge rather than a concave pocket, and the dot count must never drop to one, since a single dot beside an arc reads as Khonsu's disc at 20px. Also checked, on review, whether Sebegu's was-sceptre collides with Osiris's crossed crook-and-flail, both being staff implements — these stay distinct (a single vertical shaft versus a four-way diagonal cross) and needed no change.

**Notes from verification.** Verified against outside sources, in the order the checklist asks for. Honesty of status: six of the seven "attested" entries hold without change. Ra's disc-and-uraeus, Isis's throne-sign, and Amun's shuty are uncontested standard Egyptology. Khonsu's crescent-and-disc is confirmed as his own defining headdress specifically, not a generic lunar sign borrowed from a neighboring moon-form — it is what's shown at the Temple of Khonsu at Karnak and across New Kingdom Theban-triad reliefs. Geb's goose checks out at both levels claimed: Gardiner G38 is a biliteral "gb" goose-sign used to write his name, and independent sources describe him wearing a goose on his head as an alternative to the Red Crown. None of these needed correction. The seventh, Sebegu's was-sceptre, was already correctly marked "composed" rather than "attested," but its attestation text overclaimed: the was-sceptre in general is not Seth's exclusive attribute, it is a shared emblem of dominion carried by Set, Anubis, Khnum, and Isis alike, and by the pharaoh. What is actually Seth-specific is the canid "Set-animal" head finial this glyph depicts, not the staff shape itself — rewrote the attestation field to say that precisely instead of implying an exclusivity the sources do not support. Also surfaced, for transparency, that Mercury carries a rival Greco-Roman label, "Star of Thoth" — the proposal's Sebegu/Set identification is the older, purely Egyptian one and remains the right choice, not an omission that needs fixing. Horus the Red as Mars checked out more strongly than the proposal itself claimed: sources place "Horus the Red"/"Horus of the Horizon" for Mars as far back as the Middle Kingdom and cite the Dendera temple directly, beyond just Neugebauer & Parker. Status stays "composed" as submitted, correctly — the wedjat-eye rendering, as opposed to the generic falcon-head shown on the actual star ceilings, is still a modern simplification regardless of how old the underlying name is. The three received bodies, Nut/Uranus, Nun/Neptune, and Osiris/Pluto, all stay "composed," and none is marked attested for its planetary use. That required floor was already met and needed no correction. Wrong-culture borrowing: none found. Every attribute in this set is native Egyptian. Sebegu's borrowing from Seth is intra-pantheon, already flagged and correctly downgraded to "composed," not a foreign import from a neighboring culture. Internal distinctness: one real gap, now fixed. Khonsu's crescent-cradling-disc and Nut's dot-studded arc are both "curved line plus small round accent" shapes, and the proposal's own collision note never checked them against each other — only against the out-of-set god Yah. Fixed by tightening Nut's stroke spec (shallower curvature than Khonsu's cup, dots on the arc's convex outer edge rather than a concave pocket, dot count never dropping to one) and by adding this pairing as collision item (4). Also checked, on review, Sebegu's was-sceptre against Osiris's crossed crook-and-flail — both staff implements — and found them distinct enough (single vertical shaft vs. four-way diagonal cross) to need no change. Legibility and anachronism/offence: no failures found. Every entry stays within a roughly five-stroke budget once its own small-size fallback is applied, and nothing in the set presents a later invention as ancient or carries a modern political or religious reading.

**Within-culture collisions.** Four collision risks turned up within this set of ten (the proposal's own three, plus one this review adds), all resolvable by differentiating stroke-weight and construction rather than by picking different attributes: 1. Curved/hook family — Selene's crescent, Kronos's harpe, and Plouton's cornucopia are all, at bottom, a curved line, and a careless render collapses all three into the same crescent shape at 20px. Resolution: Selene stays a single uniform-width curve with nothing attached; Kronos gets a small inward barb plus a straight handle stroke (both load-bearing — if forced to keep only one, keep the handle); Plouton's horn is drawn with two curves for real thickness (not a thin arc) plus two small overflow dots at the mouth. If a renderer ever has to simplify one of these away, simplify Plouton's dots first, the barb on Kronos second, and never touch Selene, which is the anchor plain crescent the other two are being differentiated from. Now that Kronos's status is composed rather than attested, this is also the one place in the set where two composed/uncertain-attestation glyphs (Kronos, Plouton) sit in the same collision cluster as an attested one (Selene) — worth a second look precisely because it's also the accuracy-weakest cluster. 2. Fan-of-lines family — Helios's radiate crown and Aphrodite's shell are both a fan of short lines. Resolution: the crown's spikes rise from a distinct curved band (drawn as a visible arc beneath the spikes, like a rim); the shell's ribs converge to a single point with no band, closed by a shallow outer arc on the far side. Keep the crown's spikes even in length and the shell's ribs uneven, which also matches how each is actually drawn in the source material. 3. Headwear-on-implied-head — Helios's crown and Ares's helmet both sit on an implied brow-line. Resolution: the crown is angular and open (spikes, gap at the base); the helmet is smooth and closed (a continuous domed bowl with one curved crest arc, no spikes). These read as different enough at 20px that no further change should be needed, but it's the third-closest pair in the set and worth a designer's eye during final art. 4. Vertical-implement-with-something-at-the-top family (added on this review) — Hermes's kerykeion (shaft plus closed loop), Poseidon's trident (shaft plus three splayed tines) and, more loosely, Zeus's thunderbolt (a bolt with flared fins at both ends rather than a single shaft) all read, at a glance and at small size, as 'a stick with stuff on it.' The proposal's own collision note never addressed this family even though the brief specifically calls out staffs and spears as usual offenders. The differences already built into each construction — one closed loop, versus three separated open tines, versus flared fins at both ends of a bent spine rather than one end — are probably enough to hold apart at 20px, but this pairing went unexamined in the original submission and deserves the same designer's-eye pass given to group 3. No collision exists between Poseidon's trident and Plouton's cornucopia — the anticipated two-prong-vs-three-prong problem the brief flagged does not arise here at all, because Plouton's recommended glyph is a horn, not a bident (see that entry's rationale for why the bident was rejected on attestation grounds, independent of the legibility question).

**Notes from verification.** Corrections made on this review, in the order of the checklist: 1. HONESTY OF STATUS — Kronos (saturn) downgraded from 'attested' to 'composed.' The proposal's claim that Kronos is 'depicted wielding a sickle in Greek art from at least the 5th century BC, on coins, sculpture, relief' rested on a general myth-summary website (Mythopedia) and a personal blog (orangeoctoberskies.blogspot.com) — neither adequate sourcing for an attested claim, the strongest status this framework offers. Verification found: (a) a real academic source, 'The Sickle of Kronos' (Annual of the British School at Athens 46, 1951), expressing genuine scholarly doubt that the harpe was even sickle-shaped, weighing whether it was instead a toothed sword; (b) the one securely-identified ancient Greek coin bearing Kronos's name this review could locate (Cilicia, Mallos, 385-333 BC) shows only a bare bearded head, no sickle. What IS solidly ancient and Greek: Hesiod's Theogony gives Kronos the harpe as his castration weapon, and Kronos genuinely was a classical planet (Phainon) in Hellenistic astrology — so this is not a 'received body' problem like Uranus/Neptune/Pluto, it's a weak-visual-attestation problem. The glyph and strokes guidance are kept, since they remain the best available translation of the textual attribute into a mark; only the status and the claims backing it changed. The three received bodies (Uranus, Neptune, Pluto) were already correctly marked composed, never attested, in the original proposal — compliant, no violation there, confirmed on review. 2. WRONG-CULTURE BORROWING — the Kronos/Saturn sickle (above) is the one clear case: the vivid 'bearded elder gripping a hooked sickle' image most people actually picture is robustly attested for the Roman god Saturn (harvest and Saturnalia iconography), not confirmed for Archaic/Classical Greek Kronos specifically, and the proposal presented it as continuously and purely Greek. No other entry showed unflagged cross-culture borrowing on verification. Aphrodite's shell, though popularized further by later Roman and Renaissance art (Botticelli, ultimately), is independently confirmed as a genuine Hellenistic Greek development via the Archaeological Museum of Thessaloniki's own 300-270 BC terracotta record, so no downgrade there — the proposal's existing caveat about it being a Hellenistic-not-Archaic addition is correct and sufficient. 3. INTERNAL DISTINCTNESS — added a fourth collision group the proposal's own analysis missed: Hermes's kerykeion, Poseidon's trident and, more loosely, Zeus's thunderbolt are all 'a stick with something at the end' shapes, and the brief specifically warns that staffs are a usual offender. The proposal's existing construction differences are probably sufficient to hold them apart at 20px, but this pairing went unexamined and is now flagged for a designer's-eye pass alongside the three groups the proposal already identified. 4. LEGIBILITY — no entry exceeds roughly 3-4 strokes at full size or asks for more than the five-stroke budget; nothing flagged here. This checklist item was genuinely clean in the original proposal. 5. ANACHRONISM — two findings. First, Kronos (above). Second, minor: the Ludovisi Ares statue cited for Ares's helmet was extensively reworked by Bernini in 1622, who added the small Eros figure and the god's own right foot in fresh Carrara marble, visibly offset from the ancient Pentelic marble. The familiar 'seated god, foot on his own helmet, Eros at his feet' tableau usually reproduced in photographs is therefore partly a 17th-century Baroque invention layered onto a genuine 4th-century-BC Greek-derived original, not a clean ancient source by itself. This doesn't threaten the underlying helmet-as-Ares's-attribute claim, which independently verifies solid across Archaic/Classical vase-painting — the entry's attestation is reworded to rest its weight there rather than on the compromised statue. No offense concerns turned up in this set: nothing reads as a modern political or religious symbol by way of the shapes chosen (a plain crescent for Selene, for instance, is such an overdetermined 'moon' shape in an astrology product that it does not read as a claim about any living religious tradition). Smaller sourcing fixes, status unchanged: Hermes's entry said the snake-terminal kerykeion appears in 'Archaic and Classical' vase-painting; the actual earliest known example (a cup by the painter Macron, c. 480-470 BC) is Classical, and pre-5th-century kerykeia in Greek art end in a plain loop, not snakes — wording corrected, 'attested' status kept and, if anything, better-grounded now that it rests on a specific datable object. Poseidon's claim that vase-painting 'typically' shows a stabbing grip 'rather than raised aloft' is softened, since Poseidonia/Paestum's own coinage shows the trident raised and brandished and no fuller survey was done to confirm one grip as typical; the trident-for-Poseidon attestation itself was never remotely in doubt. Ouranos's citation swapped Grokipedia (an unvetted, AI-generated tertiary source) for Theoi.com and EBSCO Research Starters, which independently support the identical, correct claim more reliably — status unchanged, this was a sourcing-quality fix, not a factual correction. Held up verbatim on independent verification: Helios (Rhodian didrachms and the Colossus link check out), Selene (crescent diadem attested; the Parthenon caveat is correctly placed, confirmed against the actual pediment content), Zeus (Elis/Olympia thunderbolt-in-wreath coinage confirmed), and Aphrodite (the Thessaloniki figurine's date and description match the museum's own record closely). Plouton is the best-researched entry in the original set and needed no status change: its rejection of the bident as unattested — tracing the popular image to Seneca's Hercules Furens rather than to any secure Greek source, and to a misidentified vase pairing — matches what independent research turns up almost point for point, and the cornucopia-over-sceptre design choice is disclosed honestly as trading away a more common attribute (the sceptre, confirmed on verification to actually be Plouton's single most common attribute) for a rarer but better-differentiated, more thematically apt one, rather than misrepresenting which is most attested.

**Within-culture collisions.** Four shapes in this set live in a 'single curved line' family and need explicit separation rules at 20px: Yarikh's crescent (moon), El's horned crown, Kothar's fishhook, Yammu's serpent, and Motu's gaping lens all reduce to variations on a curve or pair of curves. - Yarikh vs El: Yarikh's crescent stays CLOSED (tips nearly touching, one continuous curve, no bar). El's horns stay OPEN and diverging, sitting on a straight horizontal bar — the bar is the tell at any size and must never be dropped. - Yarikh vs Kothar: the fishhook is a partial arc (~200°) with a barb tick, kept open rather than closing toward a full sickle; if the barb is lost at small size, the hook's more-open curvature is the only remaining differentiator, so don't let it tighten. - Yarikh vs Yammu: Yammu's line must show a full S-bend (two opposing curves); a single gentle arc reads as Yarikh's moon. - Yarikh vs Motu: Motu's lens must stay open at both tips (a visible gap at each end); if the tips close up it reads as Yarikh's crescent. Separately, Resheph's arrow and Baal Hadad's sprouting spear are both 'a line with something at the end(s)' and need a fixed-orientation rule to stay apart: Resheph's arrow is always diagonal with an arrowhead-plus-fletch; Baal's spear is always vertical with a leaf-fork foot. Never let either rotate into the other's angle. Attart's star and Shapash's winged disc are both circular-astral marks: Attart's star has no enclosing circle and radiates points; Shapash's disc is a small solid circle with a horizontal wing-bar and no points. Keep the star pointed and the disc round-and-barred; don't let Shapash's disc pick up rays or Attart's star pick up a circle outline. (This distinction is a design-collision rule only, unrelated to Attart's status change below — the two glyphs still need to stay apart from each other regardless of what evidence backs the star.) Two gaps this review adds, neither resolved by the rules above: - The five-way curve family (Yarikh/El/Kothar/Yammu/Motu) is only ever checked against Yarikh as the hub. There is no explicit pairwise rule for Kothar-vs-Motu, Kothar-vs-Yammu, or El-vs-Motu. The shapes look distinct on paper — a barbed hook, an open lens, an S-curve — but 'distinct from Yarikh' does not guarantee 'distinct from each other.' Test all five against each other, not just each against the crescent, before locking. - Yarikh's crescent and Attart's star sit next to each other on any real chart wheel — Moon and Venus are adjacent bodies in most zodiac/planet layouts. Neither glyph is a problem alone, but a crescent beside a star, monochrome, at small size, is also the star-and-crescent — an unintended modern political/religious reading (see notes, anachronism-and-offence). This is a whole-wheel placement risk, not a single-glyph collision, so it isn't caught by testing the two glyphs in isolation; check their actual adjacent placement in the real wheel layout before locking.

**Notes from verification.** Corrected set. The one status change: Attart's star moves from 'attested' to 'composed.' The Ugaritic-period artifact the original entry cited for it — the Minet el-Beida gold pendant, Louvre AO 14714 — shows a goddess with goats, snakes and a lion, not a star; that pendant is evidence for a different attribute (Mistress-of-Animals) that the entry's own rationale already said to leave unused, so citing it for the star was a mismatched citation, not just a thin one. The genuinely astral eight-pointed star in this general period and area belongs to an Ebla seal (c. 1725 BCE) that is explicitly Mesopotamian Ištar iconography, not native West Semitic; the star that is actually secure for Astarte specifically is Phoenician, Punic and classical, centuries after Ugaritic Bronze Age Canaan. That is exactly the evidentiary shape of Kothar's fishhook, which this set already marks 'composed' rather than 'attested' for the same reason (real attribute, same lineage, wrong period). Marking one 'composed' and the other 'attested' on matching evidence was the inconsistency; Attart now gets the same, more honest label. The star glyph itself is unchanged — it is still the right design choice, just correctly labeled as built rather than found. This was the review's central finding: a status marked 'attested' that does not hold, which the brief calls the worst defect in the set, and it does not survive. Text-only fixes, no status change: Shapash's attestation dropped an unverifiable 'cruciform' descriptor I could not confirm in the scholarship, added that Izak Cornelius's identification of the ivory figure as Shapash is explicitly tentative (not consensus), and flagged that the winged-disc device more broadly reads as a kingship/male-god emblem across the region rather than one specific to female solar deities — the Shapash-specific anchor is convention plus one tentative identification, which is thinner than 'attested' can sound but still clears the bar the same way Yarikh's does. Resheph's attestation corrected 'Ugaritic mythological fragment' to what it actually is, a ritual/incantation text (KTU 1.82), named its source reading (Fulco, accepted by Day) and flagged that the reading reconstructs a damaged line rather than being undisputed. El's attestation added that the horns on the Ras Shamra bronze survive only as drill-holes where separate horns were once attached, now lost — still a real, physical divinity marker, just more precisely described. Yarikh's attestation was strengthened, not weakened: the Hazor Stelae Temple statuette is identified by name as Yarikh specifically, not just generic crescent symbolism, which is better evidence than the original entry claimed. Confirmed compliant, no change: none of the three received/modern bodies (Shamem/Uranus, Yammu/Neptune, Motu/Pluto) are marked 'attested,' and they should not be — Shamem stays 'invented' (it is the thinnest entry in the set: a cultic-pairing name with no myth, epithet or image of its own, which is a different and thinner kind of gap than Yammu or Motu, who at least have vivid mythic language to draw glyphs from — the product's method page should say so for this entry specifically rather than folding it into the general 'received bodies are composed' disclaimer), and Yammu and Motu stay 'composed' on the strength of real, verified textual imagery (Lotan's seven heads; Mot's 'lip to the earth, lip to the heavens') with zero surviving iconography. Wrong-culture-borrowing check: the one real instance is Attart's star, addressed above (Mesopotamian Ištar, not native West Semitic, at the point it can first be documented). Everything else in the set that crosses a culture line is already handled honestly by the proposal itself: Kothar's fishhook is Phoenician not Ugaritic and is already marked 'composed'; Resheph's gazelle-horned crown is Egypt's adaptation of an imported Canaanite god (borrowing in the other direction) and the entry already sets it aside in favor of the native arrow epithet; the winged-sun-disc device (Shapash) and horned-crown-as-divinity marker (El) are both broadly shared ancient Near Eastern conventions rather than Canaan-exclusive ones, which the attestation text now says plainly instead of implying otherwise. Internal distinctness and legibility: no glyph in the set exceeds roughly three strokes at core, all ten already carry a stated small-size fallback, and the proposal's own collision notes correctly separate every pair I checked except the two gaps added under 'collisions' above (untested pairs within the five-shape curve family; the crescent-and-star placed next to each other on a real wheel). Both are testing gaps, not design failures — flag them for the at-scale test pass the proposal already calls for, rather than redesigning around them pre-emptively. Anachronism and offence: nothing else in the set reads as a later invention presented as ancient beyond what the proposal already discloses, and Baal Hadad's glyph in particular is about as solid as ancient Near Eastern iconography gets — a named, excavated, unambiguous Ugaritic depiction of the exact motif drawn. The one offence-adjacent risk is the crescent-plus-star placement noted above; it is about the wheel as a whole, not about either glyph being wrong on its own.

## Carthage

| Body | Deity | The mark | Status |
|---|---|---|---|

**Within-culture collisions.** Three real clashes were found in the record and resolved (verified in this review, all hold up): 1. Crescent-over-disc: this pairing is the single most reused Punic religious image (Tanit's fuller iconography, generic celestial stelae symbols, and Astarte's alternate horned-crescent attribute all reach for it). Resolved by never using a bare crescent-plus-disc for anyone: Semes gets a disc alone (rayed, no crescent); Tanit gets the full compound Sign of Tanit (trapezoid + crossbar + disc) rather than the crescent pairing, since the trapezoid is what makes her mark exclusive; Astarte gets a star instead of her also-attested crescent-horns variant. 2. Caduceus: genuinely attested for two figures in this culture's own record — Tanit is shown holding one on a Punic votive stele (with a dolphin, from Tophet El-Hofra/El-Horfa), confirmed independently in this review, and Sakun is textually equated with Hermes, whose defining object is the caduceus. Resolved by assigning the caduceus form to Sakun/Mercury only, on the strength of the direct god-equation, and keeping Tanit to her exclusive Sign. 3. Serpent linework: Sakun's caduceus (two snakes on a staff) and Yam's Lotan-coil (one snake, no staff) both use serpentine curves and sit two bodies apart in the list. Resolved by construction: Sakun's mark keeps a dominant vertical staff as its spine (a snake pair wrapped around a pole reads as "staff" first), while Yam's mark is a single open coil with a visible head and tapering tail and no straight element at all (reads as "creature," not "object"). The two should not be tested at 20px without checking this distinction holds. A near-miss, not a true collision, was checked and cleared: Yam's coil and the app's cothon icon (a broken ring of ship sheds around an offset island) both involve a curved, roughly circular form. The coil glyph is specified to never close into a full ring and to keep a visible head and tail breaking the circumference — this is the deliberate design constraint that keeps it from rhyming with the icon. TWO ADDITIONAL COLLISIONS FOUND IN THIS REVIEW, not caught or resolved by the original proposal: 4. Radiating-disc vs. radiating-star: Semes's sun glyph (a solid disc with four to six short rays) and Astarte's corrected eight-point star (four crossing lines through a shared center) are both, structurally, "a cluster of lines radiating from a center point" — and they now sit even closer together than in the original six-point draft, since eight points is a denser radial pattern than six. Not mentioned anywhere in the original collision analysis. Resolution: Semes must stay a solid filled disc with short blunt external ticks; Astarte must stay an open, unfilled crossing-line star with sharp points and no filled center. That fill/no-fill distinction is now load-bearing and should be spelled out as a hard rule for whoever draws the final glyphs, not left implicit. 5. Two-curves-meeting-at-a-point: Baal Hammon's ram horns (Jupiter, symmetric, closing at a brow-bridge) and Mot's jaws (Pluto, asymmetric, wide 90-120 degree gape) are both built from the same underlying construction — two curved strokes converging at a vertex. At full size they read differently (closed symmetric curl vs. open wide gape), but neither entry's own strokes note mentions the other as a risk, and both notes independently warn about degrading toward generic shapes (a hook/comma for the horns if the curl loosens; a checkmark or "v" for the jaws if the gape narrows) — which is the same failure mode converging from two directions. Recommend an explicit side-by-side legibility check at 20px for this pair specifically, plus a secondary, lower-confidence check between Mot's jaws and Baal Shamem's minimal lightning zigzag (Uranus), since a maximally-simplified version of either can reduce to "a single bent line with one or two vertices."

**Notes from verification.** CORRECTIONS MADE IN THIS REVIEW (per the brief's instruction not to soften a real problem): 1. Astarte/Venus star, points corrected from six to eight, status unchanged ("attested"). Web search confirms Astarte's Venus-star, inherited from Ishtar-Inanna, is consistently the eight-pointed star/octagram in the ancient record (Wikipedia, "Star of Ishtar"; symbolsage.com), never six-pointed. This was not a trivial stroke-count slip: a six-pointed star (hexagram) risks reading, in a product about ancient Semitic deities, as a Star of David reference — a real symbol, but a much later and culturally unrelated one, and exactly the kind of accidental modern-religious reading the brief's own anachronism/offense concern is aimed at. Fixed the glyph, rationale, attestation and strokes fields accordingly, and flagged the resulting proximity to Semes's rayed disc (see collision #4 above) since an eight-point radial pattern sits closer to a six-rayed disc than a six-point one did. 2. Saturn/Baal Hammon-the-elder sickle: softened one attestation claim that was presented in quotation marks ("his typical attribute") but could not be re-confirmed word-for-word in this review's sources, which described the North African Saturn stelae corpus as showing several possible attributes (sickle, scepter) rather than confirming the sickle as sole or universal. The underlying claim — that Saturn/Kronos iconography, sickle included, was carried into the Saturnus Africanus continuation of the Baal Hammon cult — is well supported and unchanged; only the specific quotation is walked back to a paraphrase. Status stays "attested," since the broader claim holds. 3. Added a design caution (not a status change) to the Saturn sickle entry: a lone curved-blade-on-a-handle silhouette at 20px monochrome risks a second, unwanted modern reading as the communist hammer-and-sickle emblem. The ancient Saturn/Kronos attribute is genuine and should stay; the product should just be aware of this risk when the glyph is actually drawn (keep the blade a tight crescent, never pair it near anything hammer-shaped). 4. Added two collisions the original proposal's own analysis missed (see the collisions field): Semes's rayed disc vs. Astarte's star (now closer after the eight-point correction), and the shared "two curves meeting at a vertex" construction behind Baal Hammon's horns and Mot's jaws, with a lower-confidence secondary note on Mot's jaws vs. Baal Shamem's lightning at extreme minification. CLAIMS CHECKED IN THIS REVIEW AND CONFIRMED ACCURATE, NO CHANGE NEEDED: the Sign of Tanit's hundreds-strong attestation and El-Hofra/Louvre holdings; Tanit shown holding a caduceus with a dolphin on a Punic votive stele; the Sakkun-Hermes Piraeus bilingual dedication and the debated vocalization; Baal Qarnaim and the Jebel Bou Kornine sanctuary directly across the bay from Carthage, and Baal Hammon's ram-horn iconography specifically in Carthage and North Africa; the Melqart twin-pillars tradition at Tyre and Gades (flagged as Tyre/Gades-sourced rather than Carthage-excavated, but legitimately the same cult, not a neighboring culture's borrowing); the hundreds of Saturnus Africanus stelae continuing the Baal Hammon cult under Rome; Philo of Byblos's Kronos-El-overthrows-Ouranos succession myth. All three received bodies (Uranus, Neptune, Pluto) were correctly left at "composed" in the original proposal and remain so here — none is marked attested as a planetary sign, as required. SCOPE AND HONESTY CAVEATS CARRIED FORWARD FROM THE ORIGINAL PROPOSAL, still accurate and worth keeping in front of production: - Semes and Sakun are the two weakest entries evidentially, for different reasons. Semes's glyph rests on the generic West Semitic sun-symbol convention and on plain solar discs in the Punic celestial-symbol repertoire, not on an independently rich, name-specific "Semes" cult at Carthage. Sakun's identity rests on a single bilingual inscription (Piraeus, diaspora Phoenician, not Carthage itself); the vocalization of the name is still debated in scholarship. Both are correctly marked "composed" and both would benefit from a specialist's second look. - Yam and Mot share a structural problem: both names come from the Late Bronze Age Ugaritic corpus (Ras Shamra, Syria, 14th-12th c. BC), not from Carthage's own Iron Age Punic material, and neither is clearly attested as an active cult recipient in the Carthaginian record at all. Both glyphs are built from textual epithets, not images, and both are correctly marked "composed" with that double gap stated plainly. - Punic coinage's civic emblems (horse, palm tree, ship's prow) were surveyed but not assigned to any of the ten, correctly, since they function as mint/city emblems rather than one deity's personal attribute and assigning one would manufacture a false pedigree. - Melqart's lion-skin-and-club Heracles imagery is real and well-attested on Carthaginian-minted coinage, but is itself described in the sources as an "Italianate veneer" — the twin pillars were chosen as the more indigenously Tyrian/Punic-attested option, with the club correctly noted as a legitimate, well-documented alternative if the product prefers the more recognizable Heracles read. - This pass, like the original, relied on secondary literature (Wikipedia, academic PDFs found via search, museum and encyclopedia summaries) rather than primary excavation reports or a specialist consult, and confirms the original proposal's own recommendation: a Punic-studies or Phoenician-epigraphy specialist should sanity-check the Sakun and Baal Shamem entries before production.

**Within-culture collisions.** Straight-and-curved-rod cluster: the app icon, a lituus, is a long, thin, uniformly curved shaft with a small round knop at the tip and nothing else attached. That silhouette sits in the same general territory as Mercurius's caduceus, Saturnus's harpe, and Dis Pater's key (Iuppiter's sceptre was dropped from the recommended set for this same reason). Resolution: every glyph in this cluster carries a second feature the lituus lacks. The caduceus adds wings and a crossed double-loop. The harpe is short and blade-heavy, thick-to-thin taper, visible cutting edge, rather than long and uniform. The key is drawn straight-shafted with right angles, never curved. The lituus stays the only pure single-curve wand with a plain ball terminal in the whole icon set. Bird pair, addressed directly per the brief: Iuppiter's eagle and Venus's dove would collide at 16 to 24px monochrome line weight; the anatomical differences that separate them in full art do not survive that far down. Resolved by not using either: Iuppiter takes the thunderbolt, Venus takes the mirror, and no bird attribute appears anywhere in this culture's ten glyphs. Open-curve pair: Luna's crescent and Caelus's mantle-arc are the set's only two open-curve glyphs. Differentiated by curvature and ending: Luna's crescent is a tight, closed lens with two bare pointed horns; Caelus's mantle is a shallow, wide arc with two small outward hooks at its base standing for gripped fabric, drawn noticeably wider and flatter than the crescent so the two do not read as the same mark at different rotations. Circle-based pair: Sol's radiate crown and Venus's mirror both start from a circle. Differentiated structurally, not just decoratively: Sol's circle is broken by radiating spikes around its whole edge; Venus's is a closed, unbroken ring with a single short stem beneath and nothing else. They should not be confusable even in a busy placements table. Three pairs the proposal's own collision note did not cover, added on this review: Shaft-based pair, missing from the rod cluster above: Neptunus's trident also starts from a vertical haft, which puts it in the same rough territory as the caduceus, harpe, key, and lituus, yet the original collision note never mentions it. On inspection the fork stays distinct: a crossbar with three tines fanning outward reads differently from a ball-tipped wand, a hooked blade, or a right-angled key, so no glyph change is needed. But the written analysis should say so rather than leave the trident out of a cluster it structurally belongs to. Angular-radiating pair, the closest real risk found in this review: Neptunus's trident against Iuppiter's thunderbolt. Both are built from a few short straight segments meeting at or fanning from a point, and at 16 to 24px monochrome that family resemblance is closer than the rod-cluster question above. The thunderbolt's zigzag has no crossbar and no haft; the trident's tines all point the same direction off a horizontal bar. Distinct on paper. This is the pair I would want checked side by side at production weight before shipping, not the rod cluster. Ring-on-a-stick pair: Venus's mirror against Dis Pater's key. Both are a small circle or ring terminating a straight line. In practice the mirror's disc is the large, dominant element of that glyph, while the key's ring is explicitly small and paired with a right-angle tooth the mirror has no equivalent of, so the two read as different at a glance. Flagged because the existing circle-based-pair note above compares only Sol against Venus and should be widened to say why Dis Pater's smaller ring escapes the same comparison.

**Notes from verification.** Status split unchanged from the proposal: seven attested, three composed, zero invented. None of the seven attested entries needed a downgrade on this review. Each attribute was checked against a primary or near-primary source, RIC, RRC, CRRO, OCRE, or a named ancient author, and each one belongs to the deity claimed, not to a neighboring god or a later syncretism. The three received bodies, Caelus, Neptunus, and Dis Pater, stay marked composed, and none of them crossed into attested; that rule holds. Corrections made in this review, and why: Sol: the attestation cited RIC V Aurelian 419 as showing Sol "right hand raised, globe in the left." Checked the OCRE record directly. Type 419 reads "Sol, radiate, walking left between two captives." The globe-and-raised-hand pose belongs to other numbers in the same RIC V Aurelian series. Fixed the citation; the underlying claim, that the radiate crown is the constant across the series, needed no other change. Luna: tightened the date to about 190 BC, matching the numismatic consensus that the type does not predate that year, and added two caveats: some sources catalogue the goddess as Diana rather than Luna, and later issues in the same multi-decade series add stars around the crescent, so "nothing but a small crescent" is exact for the earliest issues and not for the whole run. Venus: added a second, independent source, Philostratus the Elder's Imagines, which names the mirror as Aphrodite's attribute directly in a Roman-era text rather than only showing it in images. But flagged that this rests on wall painting, minor arts, and literary description rather than state coinage, a softer category of evidence than the coin-anchored citations for Sol, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn, worth the product team's attention if the evidentiary bar is meant to read as uniform across the set. Mars: adjusted the coin date from "about 281 BC" to "about 280 to 275 BC," matching the range given across multiple numismatic sources for this issue, and added the reverse's grain ear and the ROMANO legend for precision. Saturn: softened "brandishing the harpe overhead" to "holding the harpa and the reins," matching the catalogued pose for the Saturninus denarius; the overhead flourish was not in the source. Neptunus, the largest fix on this review: the attestation had the citation backward. It cited RRC 511 for "a bearded head of Neptune, trident behind." Checked CRRO directly. RRC 511/3a's obverse is Pompey the Great's portrait with a lituus and a jug, no trident; its reverse shows Neptune holding an aplustre, not a trident. The trident-beside-a-portrait design belongs to RRC 483 instead, and even there the portrait is catalogued as Pompey the Great, evoking his patron through the trident and dolphin placed next to him, not a bust of the god himself. The one unambiguous full figure of Neptune holding a trident in this coinage is RRC 511/4, a statue atop the Pharos of Messana. Rewrote the attestation to cite the right coin for each claim. This does not change the status: Neptunus was already marked composed for the received-body reason, correctly, since Rome never signed a planet with this trident. But a wrong citation inside a composed entry is still worth fixing, since the next person to check the source would find a coin that does not match the description. Dis Pater: checked the Pausanias passage, 5.20.3, against the Greek text directly. It holds exactly as cited, including the "locked up so none return" explanation. No change to the entry's substance. On the judgment call the proposal's own notes raised, composed versus invented for this entry: keep composed. Cicero and Varro's equation of Dis Pater with Hades and Plouton is old and stated outright in surviving Latin texts, not a modern guess, so the attribute belongs to the underworld king Rome called Dis Pater even without a Roman artifact carrying that exact Latin label next to a key. That differs in kind from an attribute with no ancient link at all, which is what the invented category should mean. The bident check confirms the proposal's own call and stands as a good catch: no ancient depiction of Hades or Pluto with a bident is secure, a kylix once read that way is now understood as Poseidon and Aethra after a flawed 19th-century restoration, and the attribute is a Renaissance convention from Raphael's workshop at the Villa Farnesina, 1517 to 1518. The method-page caveat about the Greek-source, Latin-name gap for the key needs to stay explicit either way. Wrong-culture borrowing, checked across the set: the caduceus (Hermes's kerykeion), the trident (Poseidon's), and the mirror (Aphrodite's) are all inherited from Greek iconography, but each one also turns up independently in Roman-attributed material under the Roman name: the caduceus on a Republican moneyer's own coin for Mercurius, the trident beside Pompey's portrait and on Neptune's statue for Neptunus, the mirror in Pompeian wall painting and Roman minor arts for Venus. These are shared attributes carried into Rome and then used by Romans for their own gods, not attributes borrowed from a neighbor and mislabeled as native. Dis Pater's key sits at the weak end of that same spectrum: the surviving attestation is for the Greek name, Plouton, in a Greek text, tied to the Latin name only by philosophical equation, with no Roman artifact bridging the two. Composed status and the entry's own caveat already reflect that; no glyph in this set claims a Greek attribute as though it had always been Roman. Internal distinctness: three pairs the proposal's own collision note did not cover are added to the collisions field above. Neptunus's trident against the caduceus, harpe, key, and lituus cluster: distinct in practice, but omitted from the written analysis and should be added. Neptunus's trident against Iuppiter's thunderbolt: the closest real risk found in this review, both angular marks radiating from a point, worth a side-by-side check at production weight. Venus's mirror against Dis Pater's key: distinct in practice on relative size and the key's extra right-angle tooth, but the existing circle-based-pair note should be widened to say so.
